Most weekends, you'll find me out on my bike. I've ridden all my life, for fitness and for general wellbeing. Yes, I'm a fully paid-up MAMIL (Middle-Aged Man In Lycra), and I'm happy to wear the label, Lycra and all, for what riding does for me, both physically and mentally.

It's the mental side that matters here. A few hours on the road has a way of untangling whatever has been rattling around in my head all week.

This week, that was a conversation with Scott Hamilton. I'd been showing him some of the new technology we've built at GreenCloud to harness the compute you already own, and somewhere out on the road, the idea for this post took shape.

Cast your mind back a decade or so. Cloud computing was really starting to take hold. Amazon had pushed S3 and its online compute offerings forward, and businesses were warming to a very attractive idea: making infrastructure someone else's problem.

I remember the mood amongst the developers I worked with at the time. It wasn't excitement. It was suspicion, and it centred on two things: security and control.

Judging by the scale of adoption since, the big players have done a remarkable job of talking everyone out of those reservations.

## The Great Mainframe Resale

I have a name for what has happened: the Great Mainframe Resale.

Because that is what it is. Renting time on somebody else's very large, very expensive computer is not a new idea. It is a paradigm that IBM pioneerd in the 50's that has been rebadged and sold back to business.

I'm willing to bet that at some point in the 70s or 80s, a room full of people looked at that IBM invoice and said:

> "Surely we can do this ourselves?"

I can see that moment coming around again. It is exactly why GreenCloud is positioned the way it is: to give companies back control of their computing, and to make far better use of the hardware they already own.

This is not an isolated incident, and it won't be the last.

What strikes me most is not the outage itself. Things break; that is the nature of technology. It is that the affected businesses could do **nothing** about it. 

Their entire operating model depends on infrastructure that someone else runs, and when that someone else fails, they simply wait.

As a business owner, that must be an incredibly frustrating place to be.

## Some are already walking away

Which is why I think we will see more companies extricate themselves from the cloud, at least in part.

Some already have. 37signals, the company behind Basecamp, moved off the public cloud and [expects to save around $10 million over five years](https://basecamp.com/cloud-exit). Dropbox famously did something similar back in 2016, when it [moved over 90% of its users' data out of AWS](https://dropbox.tech/infrastructure/magic-pocket-infrastructure) and onto its own custom-built infrastructure.

But this isn't only about cost. It's about responsibility.

## Read the small print

We are encouraged to believe that our data is safe in the hands of Amazon, Google and Microsoft, some of the largest and most sophisticated technology companies in the world.

But how many businesses have actually read the agreements they've signed?

Here is the gist, with links so you can check for yourself.

**Amazon Web Services** is remarkably explicit. Its [Customer Agreement](https://aws.amazon.com/agreement/) provides services "as is", disclaims any warranty that your content will be secure or won't be lost, makes you responsible for securing and backing up your own data, and generally caps its liability at what you paid in the previous twelve months.

**Google Cloud** takes a similar line. Its [Terms of Service](https://cloud.google.com/terms) disclaim guarantees of uninterrupted or error-free operation and, again, generally limit liability to twelve months of fees. Its [Service Specific Terms](https://cloud.google.com/terms/service-terms) add further restrictions on some services.

**Microsoft Azure** varies depending on how you buy it, but the principle is the same. Business customers are covered by Microsoft's [licensing terms](https://www.microsoft.com/licensing/terms/), which contain their own limits on liability and responsibility.

None of these agreements tells you not to put important data in the cloud. But all three are very clear about how little of the risk they are prepared to carry.

Look closely at that twelve-month cap, because it is measured in the wrong currency. It is based on what you paid *them*, not on what you stand to lose.

So ask yourself: what does your business spend on cloud services in a year? Now, what would it cost you if your systems went down for a day? A week? If you couldn't take payments, serve your customers or keep your staff working?

For most businesses, those two numbers are nowhere near each other. A company spending £50,000 a year on cloud hosting could easily turn over that much in a matter of days.

And even that cap flatters the position. AWS and Google both exclude lost revenue and profits altogether, and AWS explicitly excludes the value of your content. The money you lose while you can't trade is, contractually, your problem.

## Outsourcing infrastructure is not outsourcing risk

This is the contradiction at the heart of how most people think about the cloud.

Businesses move to the cloud believing they have handed responsibility to a specialist. In reality, they have handed over the *management* of their infrastructure while keeping the *responsibility* for their data, their backups and their ability to keep trading.

Which raises an obvious question:

**If your business is ultimately responsible for its data, shouldn't it have more control over where that data lives and how it is protected?**

The public cloud is an excellent solution for many workloads; I'm not pretending otherwise. But when it goes wrong, and it does, a business that relies on it entirely has very few options. The agreements it freely signed allow the provider to operate exactly this way, while the business absorbs the losses.

## AI: the same model, only bigger

And then there's AI.

It is an amazing technology. But almost all of it is being built on exactly the model we've just been talking about: compute centralised in a handful of enormous data centres, owned by a handful of enormous companies.

The appetite for those data centres is staggering, and the costs aren't only financial. Earlier this month in northern Finland, Google was [ordered to stop work on two data centre sites](https://www.theguardian.com/environment/ng-interactive/2026/oct/22/finland-google-felling-trees-datacentre) after clearing an estimated 250,000 trees from 330 hectares of forest without first completing the required environmental impact assessments. The sites are part of a €13bn AI infrastructure project. Google has apologised, saying it acted on "the best understanding we had of the legislation at the time".

That is the true cost of making AI someone else's problem: more concentration, more dependence and more pressure on the planet.

And yet something interesting is happening at the same time. Capable AI models are starting to run well on ordinary devices: the laptops, desktops and workstations that businesses already own. Not every workload, and not the very largest models, but a growing share of the genuinely useful ones.

Which brings us back to the same question. If the compute is already sitting on your desks, why send the work, and your data, somewhere else?

## Taking back control

There is another approach.

Keep the capability to run on infrastructure you own and control, so that when a third party falls over, you don't fall over with it. A fallback that keeps you operational, rather than leaving you watching a status page and waiting.

That is what GreenCloud is built for. It puts the computers, servers and GPUs you already own to work, keeps your data inside your own environment, and means you aren't wholly dependent on someone else's data centre. And making better use of the hardware that already exists is far kinder to the planet than feeding the demand for ever more of it.

The cloud sold us convenience. What it quietly took in return was control.

Wouldn't it be a sensible idea to take some back?
